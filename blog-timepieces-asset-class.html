Timepieces as an Asset Class: What Collectors Should Know Before Buying

Published 31 July 2026

A handful of watch references have outperformed major equity indices over the past two decades. That fact alone has pulled fine timepieces into conversations once reserved for real estate and fine art — but treating a watch purely as an investment misunderstands both the asset and the market.

Value Drivers Are Different From Other Luxury Assets

Unlike real estate, a timepiece's value is not primarily driven by location or square footage — it is driven by a combination of rarity, condition, provenance, and brand-specific demand dynamics that shift over time. A reference that is highly sought after today may not hold the same position in five years; conversely, references overlooked by the broader market can appreciate sharply once collector attention turns to them.

Rarity and production numbers. Limited production runs, discontinued references, and unusual dial or case variations typically command premiums — but rarity alone does not guarantee value; it must be rarity the market actually wants.

Condition and originality. Unpolished cases, original dials, and complete original components (box, papers, links) materially affect value — often more than most first-time buyers expect. A watch that has been over-polished or has replacement parts can lose a significant percentage of its value versus an all-original example, even if the difference is invisible to an untrained eye.

Provenance. Documented ownership history, original invoices, and service records at authorized centers all support both value and — critically — authenticity.

Brand and reference dynamics. Certain manufacturers maintain long waiting lists at retail, which pushes secondary market premiums on specific references; others see values driven more by collector sentiment than retail scarcity. These dynamics require ongoing market attention, not a one-time assessment.

Authentication Is Non-Negotiable

The secondary watch market has a well-documented counterfeit and fraud problem, ranging from outright fakes to "franken-watches" assembled from mismatched genuine parts. For any transaction of consequence, authentication should involve:

  • Verification against manufacturer archives or extract confirmation, where available
  • Physical inspection by an independent, watch-specific expert — not a general jeweler
  • Movement inspection, not just case and dial verification

Skipping this step to close a transaction faster is the single most common way collectors lose value, not gain it.

Timing and Liquidity Considerations

Unlike public equities, the timepiece secondary market has no continuous, transparent pricing — comparable sales data exists, but true liquidity depends on finding the right buyer, not simply listing at a market price. This makes discretion and network access, rather than public marketplaces, the more reliable path for both acquisition and eventual resale of significant pieces.
